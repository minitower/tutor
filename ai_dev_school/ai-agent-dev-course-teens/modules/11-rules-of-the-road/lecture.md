# Lecture Script — Module 11: Rules of the Road

Facilitator notes: this is a spoken script, not a script to read word for
word. Say it like you'd explain it to a smart 15-year-old who already
knows using AI to code is normal and just wants to know what actually
matters here. Total lecture portion should run **~20-25 minutes**; the
rest of the 45-90 minute module is the hands-on task and checkpoint.
Slide numbers below match `slides.md` exactly. Keep the tone practical,
not preachy — this is "here's why this protects you," not a lecture
about cheating.

---

## Opening (~1 min)

## Slide 1 — Rules of the Road

Welcome them in. Something like:

"We've spent ten modules getting you actually good at directing an AI
agent — specs, plans, tests, review, all of it. Today's module is
different. It's not a new technical skill. It's about something that
matters just as much: how you use all of that, and whether you're
honest about it. Nobody's going to hand you a rulebook for this. You're
going to write your own, today, and I'm going to tell you why that's
worth taking seriously."

---

## Slide 2 — Today

"Here's where we're headed. Nobody's shocked anymore that you used an
agent — that's not the interesting question. The interesting question
is *how*, and there's a real line between directing the work well and
not actually understanding what you turned in. We'll look at a few
places where you're expected to be upfront about AI help — school,
portfolio, open source. Then you're writing your own AI use policy, and
your checkpoint is that policy plus one sentence on *why* each rule
exists."

*(~3 min total so far)*

---

## The Big Idea (~6-7 min)

## Slide 3 — Using AI Help Is Normal Now

"Let's start by clearing something up, because I don't want today to
land as 'here comes the lecture about cheating.' It's not that. Using an
agent to help you build something is completely normal now. Nobody's
impressed or scandalized that you did. That ship has sailed, and it's
not coming back.

So if that's not the question anymore, what is? It's this: how did you
use it, and are you honest about that? That's the whole module in one
sentence."

## Slide 4 — The Line Only You Can Draw

"Here's the line that actually matters: 'I directed this well' versus
'I don't actually understand what I turned in.' And here's the annoying
part — both of those can produce code that runs, looks clean, and passes
whatever checks you throw at it. From the outside, they can look
identical.

But only one of them means you can explain it later, extend it when the
assignment changes, or fix it when it breaks at 11pm the night before
it's due. And here's the thing nobody else can do for you: draw that
line. Not your supervisor, not a teacher, not me. They can ask you
questions that reveal which side you're on — but only you actually know,
in the moment, whether you understood what just happened or just
clicked approve because it looked fine."

## Slide 5 — Two Different Questions

"Split it into two separate questions, because people mix them up
constantly. Question one: does it work? The agent is genuinely good at
helping you answer that — tests pass, it runs, the output looks right.
Question two: do *I* actually understand what I turned in? That one, the
agent cannot answer for you. Only you can.

And here's the trap — a polished, working result can quietly hide a 'no'
on that second question. It ran. It looked great. And you still
couldn't explain three lines of it if someone asked. This module is
really just about not lying to yourself on that second question."

*(~10 min total so far)*

---

## Why This Actually Matters For You (~3 min)

## Slide 6 — Why This Actually Matters For You

"I want to be direct about why I'm spending class time on this instead
of just trusting you to figure it out. Three reasons, and none of them
are 'because cheating is bad.'

Portfolio: if you show this project to a college or in a job interview,
someone may ask you to explain a piece of it, live, on the spot. If you
can't, that's not a great moment.

Skill: this whole course exists to build a real skill in you — directing
an agent well, but also still being able to reason about code yourself.
If you never do that reasoning, you don't build the skill you actually
came here for, no matter how many working projects you produce.

Trust: get caught misrepresenting what you did — once — and every future
claim you make gets questioned. That's expensive, and it's hard to earn
back. This isn't about morality. It's about protecting your own future
options."

*(~13 min total so far)*

---

## Disclosure Norms (~7-8 min)

## Slide 7 — Disclosure Norm: School

"Let's get concrete with three places this shows up, starting with
school. Policies here vary a *lot* — some classes flat-out ban AI use,
some want you to cite it like a source, some genuinely don't care. There
is no single universal rule.

Here's the trap: 'I didn't think to mention it' is not the same thing as
'I checked and it was fine.' Those feel similar in the moment but
they're not the same defense. If you're not sure what a class's policy
is, ask the teacher *before* you turn the assignment in — not after
something goes wrong."

## Slide 8 — Disclosure Norm: Portfolio

"Next: your portfolio. A portfolio is a claim — it's you saying 'I can
do this.' Using AI help to build what's in it is completely reasonable;
professionals building real products do it too, constantly.

What's not reasonable is presenting code an agent wrote, that you
genuinely can't explain, as proof of a skill you don't actually have.
That's not a small technicality — that's the portfolio not doing its
one job, which is telling the truth about what you can do.

And here's a thing that surprises people: a short, honest note — 'built
with Claude Code — I designed the data model and debugged the sync bug
myself' — reads as *more* credible than staying silent, not less. It
shows you know exactly what you contributed."

## Slide 9 — Disclosure Norm: Open Source

"Third place: open source. A lot of projects now have explicit,
written policies about AI-assisted contributions — read them before you
open a pull request, the same way you'd read any other contributing
guide.

Why do maintainers care so much? Because what they actually need is
someone who can answer questions about the code later — when a bug
shows up, when someone asks 'why did you do it this way.' Sending in
code you can't discuss or defend doesn't just waste your time if it
gets rejected. It wastes the maintainer's time too, reviewing something
nobody can actually stand behind."

## Slide 10 — Nobody Polices This For You

"Step back for a second and notice something uncomfortable: nobody is
really watching this closely. A teacher can't monitor every keystroke
you typed. A portfolio reviewer can't see your process, only the
result. And it's only getting harder to check from the outside — the
tools are going to keep getting better at hiding the seam between 'the
AI did this part' and 'I did this part.'

Which means the decision is basically yours by default, whether you
make it on purpose or not. Deciding your own rules now, while the
stakes are just a school project, is a much better time to figure this
out than under real pressure later — a job, a client, something with
actual consequences riding on it."

*(~21 min total so far)*

---

## Handing Off to the Hands-On Task (~3-4 min)

## Slide 11 — Your Turn: Hands-On

"Okay, here's what you're actually doing. Write a short AI use policy —
a page or less, this isn't an essay — covering three things.

One: when you'll use an agent for a project, and when you'll
deliberately do something yourself even if the agent could do it
faster. Two: how you'll disclose AI help when it's relevant — a class
project, a portfolio note, just telling a teammate. Three: what you will
never skip doing yourself, even in a hurry — the understanding part,
not just 'does it run.'

This is yours. There's no single correct policy — mine would look
different from yours, and that's fine."

## Slide 12 — Make It Yours, Not Generic

"One warning before you start writing, because I've seen where this
goes wrong. 'I'll be honest about it' is not a rule. It sounds like one,
but it doesn't tell you what to actually *do* in the moment when you're
tired, behind schedule, and tempted to just ship it.

A real rule is specific enough that you could actually follow it under
those conditions. Here's an example of what specific looks like: 'If I
can't explain a function out loud, I rewrite it myself before
submitting.' That's a rule you can check yourself against at 11pm. 'Be
honest' is not."

*(~25 min total so far — hands-on task happens now, live, not scripted
here)*

---

## Checkpoint and Wrap-Up (~2-3 min, after hands-on is done)

## Slide 13 — Checkpoint

"When you're done writing, here's your checkpoint, and don't skip the
second part — it's the one that actually matters. Turn in the policy
itself, and next to *each* rule, one sentence explaining why it's
there — not just restating what the rule says in different words.

Here's the tell I'm going to be looking for: if you can't explain why a
rule exists, you probably copied it from somewhere instead of actually
deciding it for yourself. That's the whole point of this exercise — not
having a policy, but having *your* policy, for reasons you can state."

## Slide 14 — Recap

"Let's lock in today's big things. Using AI help is normal — what's
actually being judged is how you use it and whether you disclose it.
'Does it work' and 'do I understand it' are different questions, and a
good answer to one can quietly hide a bad answer to the other. Disclosure
norms differ by context — school, portfolio, open source — so check,
don't assume. And rules you decide now, on purpose, beat rules you
improvise later under pressure."

## Slide 15 — Next Up

"Next time is the capstone — Module 12, build and show. You're putting
everything from this whole course together on one real project you
actually want to finish: spec, plan, tests, a second-agent review, and a
walkthrough where you explain what you built versus what the agent did.
Everything we did today about honesty and understanding is about to get
tested for real, on something that's actually yours."
