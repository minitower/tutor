# Rules of the Road

Module 14 — Build With AI

---

<!-- Slide 2 -->

## Today

- Why *how* you used AI matters as much as whether it worked
- The line between "I directed this well" and "I don't actually understand what I turned in"
- A few concrete disclosure norms: school, portfolio, open source
- Hands-on: write your own AI use policy
- Checkpoint: the policy, plus *why* each rule is there

---

<!-- Slide 3 -->

## Using AI Help Is Normal Now

- Nobody's shocked anymore that you used an agent to help build something
- That's not really the question anymore
- The question is: how did you use it, and are you honest about that

---

<!-- Slide 4 -->

## The Line Only You Can Draw

- "I directed this well" vs. "I don't actually understand what I turned in"
- Both can produce code that runs and looks fine
- Only one of them means you can explain it, extend it, or fix it later
- Nobody else can draw this line for you — not your supervisor, not your teacher

---

<!-- Slide 5 -->

## Two Different Questions

- "Does it work?" — the agent can help you answer that pretty reliably
- "Do I actually understand what I turned in?" — only you can answer that
- A polished result can quietly hide a "no" on the second question
- This module is about not lying to yourself on that one

---

<!-- Slide 6 -->

## Why This Actually Matters For You

- Not a lecture about cheating — a practical thing that protects *you*
- **Portfolio:** someone may ask you to explain a piece of it in an interview
- **Skill:** if you never do the reasoning yourself, you don't build the skill you came here for
- **Trust:** get caught misrepresenting what you did once, and every future claim gets questioned

---

<!-- Slide 7 -->

## Disclosure Norm: School

- Policies vary a lot — some classes ban AI use, some require you to cite it, some don't care
- "I didn't think to mention it" isn't the same as "I checked and it was fine"
- When in doubt: ask the teacher *before* you turn it in, not after

---

<!-- Slide 8 -->

## Disclosure Norm: Portfolio

- A portfolio is a claim: "I can do this"
- Using AI help is reasonable — professionals do it too
- Not reasonable: presenting agent-written code you can't explain as proof of a skill you don't actually have
- A short, honest note ("built with Claude Code — I designed X and debugged Y") reads as *more* credible, not less

---

<!-- Slide 9 -->

## Disclosure Norm: Open Source

- Many projects now have explicit AI-contribution policies — read them before you open a pull request
- Maintainers care whether someone can answer questions about the code later
- Submitting code you can't discuss or defend wastes a maintainer's time, not just yours

---

<!-- Slide 10 -->

## Nobody Polices This For You

- A teacher can't watch every keystroke; a portfolio reviewer can't see your process
- The tools will only get better at hiding the seam between "AI did it" and "I did it"
- Deciding your own rules now, while the stakes are a school project, beats figuring it out later under real pressure

---

<!-- Slide 11 -->

## Your Turn: Hands-On

Write a short "AI use policy" — a page or less — covering:

1. When you'll use an agent, and when you'll deliberately do something yourself even if the agent's faster
2. How you'll disclose AI help when it's relevant (class project, portfolio note, telling a teammate)
3. What you'll never skip doing yourself — the understanding part, not just "does it run"

---

<!-- Slide 12 -->

## Make It Yours, Not Generic

- "I'll be honest about it" isn't a rule — it doesn't tell you what to actually *do* in the moment
- A real rule is specific enough to follow when you're tired or behind schedule
- Specific example: "If I can't explain a function out loud, I rewrite it myself before submitting"

---

<!-- Slide 13 -->

## Checkpoint

- The policy itself
- One sentence next to **each** rule explaining *why* it's there — not just restating the rule
- If you can't explain why a rule exists, you probably copied it instead of deciding it

---

<!-- Slide 14 -->

## Recap

- Using AI help is normal — how you use it and whether you disclose it is what's actually being judged
- "Does it work" and "do I understand it" are different questions — don't let a good answer to one hide a bad answer to the other
- Disclosure norms differ by context (school, portfolio, open source) — check, don't assume
- Rules you decide now, on purpose, beat rules you improvise later under pressure
